# Pick HeatGuard, pivot to tomorrow's outreach

**Tackle B1 HeatGuard.** Frame it as a next-day heat-risk forecast that produces a ranked "who to check on tomorrow" list for eldercare and grassroots teams, not as a live temperature dashboard. On our weighted scoring, B1 comes first in both rounds: **4.60/5 for Round 1 and 4.80/5 for Demo Day**, ahead of A1 SilverWatch (4.25 in both rounds). B1 is the only problem where all of the following hold. First, an official, live, forecastable hazard signal exists: NEA's **WBGT heat-stress API reports from 30 stations every 15 minutes, with history back to February 2025** ([data.gov.sg WBGT](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_87884af1f85d702d4f74c6af13b4853d/view)). Second, planning-area demographics exist to cross it with. Third, it aligns with two 2026 headline priorities: the Year of Climate Adaptation, whose heat-resilience R&D programme names the elderly as a priority group, and Singapore turning "super-aged" ([MSE YOCA](https://www.mse.gov.sg/yoca/); [Seasia](https://seasia.co/2026/03/26/singapore-2026-navigating-the-silent-crisis-of-a-super-aged-society)). Fourth, we found no Singapore student project that combines NEA real-time temperature with elderly demographics, which matters because originality carries 30% of the Round 1 score. The team should flag these errors up front, because correcting them earns data-rigour credit. B1's one serious risk is infrastructure. Nobody has confirmed that Databricks Free Edition can reach data.gov.sg APIs, so test that this week, before the 6 October idea deadline. The runner-up is **A1 SilverWatch**. It has the same social story, needs no live API, and is the fallback if outbound calls turn out to be blocked. Avoid C1 FlatFair (heavily saturated) and A2 DengueRadar (the open dengue series ends in 2022 and 2026 case counts are at a multi-year low).

## The winning angle is a next-day outreach roster built on NEA's own heat bands

**Pitch line:** *"Tomorrow afternoon, which 20 subzones have the most elderly, low-income or mobility-limited residents facing High heat stress, and who should knock on their doors today?"* The user is a named operator, such as an Active Ageing Centre coordinator, a Silver Generation Office ambassador or a Town Council. The decision is concrete: who to visit or call before the heat peaks, and which cooling refuge to point them to. This answers the organisers' tip to "make it actionable: who would use this, and what decision does it inform?" ([submission checklist](../docs/guidelines/submission_and_demo-checklist.txt)). It also answers the 30% originality criterion, because the headline output is a ranked action list, not a dashboard.

**Pipeline and ML design.** A scheduled Databricks Job polls WBGT every 15 minutes (its native cadence) and temperature/RH every 5–15 minutes, landing raw JSON in a Unity Catalog Volume. **One Lakeflow Declarative Pipeline** then runs Auto Loader into bronze, parses and deduplicates into silver with data-quality expectations (null station IDs, physically impossible readings, duplicate timestamps), and builds gold tables. The single pipeline respects Free Edition's one-active-pipeline-per-type limit ([Databricks docs](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/getting-started/free-edition-limitations)). Spatial SQL and H3 functions, now GA on serverless with native AI/BI maps, interpolate station readings to subzone centroids using inverse-distance weighting and compute distance to the nearest cooling refuge ([Databricks Spatial SQL GA](https://www.databricks.com/blog/geospatial-unbounded-spatial-sql-ga-aibi-maps-delta-sharing-and-iceberg-v3)).

The model layer has two parts, both tracked in MLflow. **(1) A next-day peak-WBGT forecast per station.** It is a gradient-boosted model on lagged WBGT, temperature, RH and wind plus NEA's 24-hour and 4-day forecast values, trained on February 2025 to September 2026. It is benchmarked against persistence ("tomorrow = today") and a climatology baseline. As a stretch, a WBGT emulator from temperature/RH/wind can extend the training history back to 2016 using the yearly CSVs. **(2) A Heat Vulnerability Index per subzone.** This is a transparent composite: z-scored 65+ share, 1-person-household share, ADL-difficulty share, 1–2-room HDB share and refuge distance. Weights are shown with a sensitivity analysis, and PCA serves as a cross-check. **Risk = forecast hazard band × vulnerability rank**, bucketed into Low/Medium/High using NEA's own heatStress bands for the hazard axis.

The gold outputs feed four surfaces. An **AI/BI dashboard** shows the map, the live station view and the ranked roster. A **Genie space** lets a coordinator ask questions such as "Which Bedok subzones are High tomorrow and more than 800 m from a community club?" A **Foundation Model API step** drafts plain-language advisories in the subzone's dominant language, using the Census language table. A **Databricks App** is optional and would give a one-screen coordinator view. For the 20% UX criterion, the non-technical user should see a single ranked list with a reason code per row, for example "High WBGT forecast + 23% living alone + 1.1 km to nearest CC".

**Data volume and backfill fit comfortably.** WBGT's 96 daily timestamps paginate at 25 per call, so the full February 2025 to now history comes to roughly 2,400 calls. That is about an hour at the no-key limit of 6 calls per 10 seconds ([data.gov.sg rate limits](https://guide.data.gov.sg/developer-guide/api-overview/api-rate-limits)); the call count is our estimate from the observed pagination. Temperature at 1-minute resolution is heavier, around 36,000 calls for 2025–26. Pull 2016–2024 from the CSVs, and for 2025 onward either sample hourly or use a Dev/Prod API key.

## Honest limitations become points if they are named first

The data-rigour criterion explicitly rewards "honest limitations" ([judging criteria](../docs/guidelines/judging-criteria.txt)). B1 has five, and each has a clear fix. **Spatial sparsity:** 30 WBGT and 16 temperature stations cover 55 planning areas, so many subzones are interpolated, not measured. Show the distance to the nearest station as a confidence field. **No outcome labels:** heat illness is published only as national totals (ED cases rose from 280 in 2024 to 398 in 2025) ([Yahoo/ST](https://sg.news.yahoo.com/researchers-mission-improve-heat-illness-210000050.html)). The "Heat Risk Score" is therefore a forecast crossed with a composite index, not a supervised classifier, and the team should say so. **Stale demographics:** Census 2020 is six years old. SingStat's June 2025 planning-area/subzone file would refresh it, but it is not on data.gov.sg and its page returned a 404 in testing ([SingStat file](https://www.singstat.gov.sg/-/media/files/find_data/population/statistical_tables/respopagesextod2025e.ashx)). **Refuges:** the only eldercare point layer predates the 2021 AAC rebrand, since 133 SAC points compare with 223+ AACs today. Lean on CCs, hawker centres and parks, and label the 2016 layer as historical. **Short WBGT history:** about 19 months is enough to train a next-day model, but it covers only one full hot season. Report baseline comparisons, not headline accuracy.

## Runner-up A1, and the three problems to avoid

**Runner-up: A1 SilverWatch.** Choose it if check 1 fails and the team does not want to rely on an off-platform poller. It is also the right choice if the team is stronger at analytics than data engineering. The winning A1 angle is small-area estimation: apply national 65+ living-alone ratios by age and sex (`d_45acf574880ac493ec638f72338e62d5`) to Census 2020 subzone age/sex structure, cross-check against the 1-person-household and ADL-difficulty proxies, and produce an **AAC outreach roster** rather than a heatmap. Spatial SQL coverage gaps measure 65+ residents beyond 400 m or 800 m of a centre. National policy already says 8 in 10 seniors have an AAC nearby, so the gap analysis targets the remaining 2 in 10 ([Age Well SG](https://www.agewellsg.gov.sg/about/)). Its limits: there is no isolation ground truth, and current AAC coordinates must be compiled by hand from AIC listings of about 230–250 centres ([AIC](https://www.aic.sg/care-services/active-ageing-centres)). A Lakebase-backed "visit logged" app would be its Databricks differentiator.

**Avoid** C1, A2 and B2. C1 FlatFair has the cleanest data in the challenge, but it will face look-alike submissions from teams reusing well-known projects, and it scores lowest on originality. A2 DengueRadar can credibly forecast only *national* weekly cases. The open series ends in 2022, 2023 onward must be stitched from CDA bulletins, and 2026 incidence is too low for the brief's "tens of thousands of cases" framing ([data.gov.sg](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_ca168b2cb763640d72c4600a68f9909e/view)). B2 FloodSense asks for a supervised classifier, but only about 11 months of rare flood alerts and four national hectare figures are available as labels ([data.gov.sg](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_c4aed98f1533eb3a66f65dbb1a30da46/view)).

## Conclusion

The deciding factor in this challenge is not topic appeal. It is whether the brief's *target variable* exists in open data at the granularity the demo promises. By that test most briefs fail somewhere, and B1 fails only on inputs that can be proxied, while its hazard signal is official, live and forecastable. That lets a team present its handling of the brief's wrong dataset list as rigour rather than excuse, which is a real asymmetry in a field where many teams will copy the "winning demo" bullets verbatim.

The larger strategic insight is that the ageing and heat problems are the same problem viewed from two sides. The strongest submission fuses them, putting A1's vulnerability proxies under B1's live, forecastable hazard, and aims the output at a named operator making a decision the next morning. If the egress test passes this week, B1 is the pick. If it fails and a laptop poller is unacceptable, reuse the same vulnerability pipeline as A1 and lose little.
